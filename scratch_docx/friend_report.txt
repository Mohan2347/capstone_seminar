CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

1.1 OVERVIEW

Urban infrastructure is an aspect of urban life that most individuals only become concerned with once it stops functioning properly. An ignored pothole turns into an actual threat to motorists and passers-by. A faulty light post results in a section of the road becoming unsafe at night. The garbage pileup that occurs in the designated location becomes an issue because there was no collection over the holidays. None of these are sensational events. They are common examples of how infrastructure issues affect residents on a daily basis.

The fundamental problem is not the presence of these issues, but rather the inefficiency with which cities address them. Complaint portals operate in a way similar to the following: A citizen finds the complaint portal online, makes his/her complaint in writing, sends it and waits. The complaint is received into the system where it sits in a queue until some time later when a human being reads it, classifies it into the right category and sends it to the proper department to schedule a response to the issue. Each link in that chain needs the availability of humans, and the chain will break whenever that availability is not available after hours, on weekends, with understaffing, or where the number of calls exceeds the capacity of the staff to handle them.

The reason we created AEUGS was our realization of this gap and capability to fill it using technology. All that it takes for a citizen to report a concern is a picture, a brief description, and their location coordinates; and the process after it is fully automated. From the minute that the issue is received, the system will scan, analyze it, prioritize it, cross-reference it with other complaints in the area, and route it to the proper department for action there’s no need to wait for a clerk to read it nor for a complaint to go unnoticed on the weekend.

This intelligent process is made possible by Google Gemini 1.5 Flash, an AI capable of analyzing both an image and a textual description the way that an inspector would assess an observation and a verbal report and come up with one conclusion. This conclusion includes the category of the problem, severity of the problem, time sensitivity, and confidence level of the machine. This consistency does not change according to the time period at which the complaint has been received, or even the person who has filed the complaint.

In addition to categorizing and prioritizing them, we have developed an innovative algorithm called Multimodal Civic Intelligence Algorithm (MCIA), which assigns scores to different complaints depending upon their impact and not their time of submission. The impact depends on the assessment done using artificial intelligence along with other factors like how urgent the situation is and how many times the complaint has been voted on.

The platform is live at https://ai-civic-issue.vercel.app/ and the source code is publicly available at https://github.com/Guna444/AI-Based-Civic-Issue-Reporting-System.

1.2 OBJECTIVE OF THE PROJECT

This project mainly aims to build an intelligent full-stack web application for integrated urban civic complaint management system. The specific objectives are:

How we are using Artificial Intelligence to classify and prioritize civic complaints The system reads the incoming complaints and classifies them to a relevant civic department, and then also assigns priority on a scale based on how urgent or impactful that complaint is. This lowers manual work and ensures that priority problems are promptly addressed without always relying on human intervention.

Using transparency and fewer duplicates to increase citizen-government engagement Citizens will track their complaints in real-time, as well as view administrator notes made by the AI on decisions about their complaint. It also uses geospatial de-duplication techniques to identify and merge duplicate complaints by their geographic proximity, removing noise from the complaint records, thus improving the civic authorities overall responsiveness.

To create measurable performance metrics to hold admins accountable and track the resolution The system has a scoring system in place that helps maintain timelines of complaint resolution on administrators. All this data allows governing bodies to assess efficiency, find points of congestion and uphold a higher minimum standard of civic service delivery.





1.3 DESCRIPTION OF THE PROJECT

The platform consists of two kinds of users – citizens who report and monitor their complaints, and administrators who check, assign, and solve complaints. Citizens engage with a two-stage reporting interface where they input information about their complaint such as its title, description, pictures, and their location. Administrators work with a dashboard that shows them a live priority list, a searchable list of issues, and visual charts showing analytics. 

From the moment of submission, each issue goes through an AI pipeline: Google’s Gemini scans text and images simultaneously, classifies the issue into one of ten civic categories, assigns severity and urgency levels ranging from 1 to 4 each, calculates confidence rate, and writes a brief explanation. Then the MCIA algorithm calculates a priority score from 0 to 10. Should there have been a complaint with the same issue, category, and location reported within the 500-meter radius before, it will be considered a duplicate and not create a separate administrator issue but simply link with an existing one.

1.4 SCOPE OF THE PROJECT

The scope includes everything in the complaints management process, which starts from when the complaint is made by a citizen together with its picture and geolocation data, followed by its classification using Google’s Gemini 1.5 Flash AI, duplicate geospatial detection through the Haversine formula, its prioritization according to the Multi Criteria Intelligence Assessment model with real-time re-prioritization, admin approval using a protected dashboard, status update in six stages of its lifecycle, and lastly its resolution.

1.5 USE CASE MODEL

There are three levels of stakeholders in the system. The Citizen stakeholder (who is authenticated by a username/password combination) can create complaints, attach pictures to a complaint, associate a complaint with GPS location information, rate other people’s complaints, and monitor his/her own complaints. The Administrator stakeholder (who is protected with a role) can access complaints in the priority list, filter/sort through all complaints, modify the status of complaints, assign complaints to departments, write resolutions, and access analytics reports.



1.6 SYSTEM DESCRIPTION

The stack is a web application built on Next.js15 and follows the App Router architecture, utilizing server-side rendering, TypeScript, and API routes for all backend logic. Authentications is done using Clerk, where webhook syncs ensure that there is a database entry for each and every user in Clerk. The database used is PostgreSQL on Neon DB (serverless) and access is through Prisma ORM. AI classification takes place in an isolated Python Fast API microservice.

1.7 CUSTOMER AND USER PROFILES

Primary Users: The primary user group includes the people who have witnessed an issue with their local infrastructure and wish to report it. These people need not necessarily be very technically literate; the design of the system is such that they can use it even without any prior guidance. Secondary Users: These include municipal officials, facility managers, or faculty (if used within an organization). They should be digitally literate but do not require any particular technical expertise.

1.8 ASSUMPTIONS AND DEPENDENCIES

The software expects the citizens to have a contemporary web browser and a photo-taking gadget. Geographical location detection relies on the geolocation feature of the browser, but one can manually input the coordinates as an alternative. Outbound internet connectivity must be present for accessing the Gemini API services. The system needs the Gemini 1.5 Flash API from Google, the NeonDB serverless PostgreSQL database, Clerk authentication service, and the Vercel platform. These services are provided by their respective providers at the agreed service levels.

1.9 FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS

Citizens are able to register and authenticate themselves through either email/ password authentication or through social accounts.

Citizens are able to lodge complaints with a title, description, image(s) (one or multiple), and GPS location.

Each complaint will be automatically categorized by Google Gemini 1.5 Flash multimodal classifier.

Every complaint is assigned a priority rating of LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH, or CRITICAL as well as a severity rating of LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH, or IMMEDIATE.

Duplication of complaints is detected using the Haversine geospatial distance algorithm within 500m.

Each citizen’s complaint gets an MCIA Priority score of 0 to 10 when lodged.

Citizens are allowed to upvote open complaints; each upvote changes the priority score in real-time.

Citizens can use the live tracker interface to monitor complaint statuses.

Administrators can access the priority-sorted list of all open complaints.

Administrators can filter complaints based on status, type, and severity while sorting them by priority, number of upvotes, or time of registration.

Administrators have the ability to update statuses, enter comments, and assign complaints to departments.

Administrators are provided with analytics in form of pie charts showing monthly trends, categories, and severities.

1.10 NON-FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS

Classification should take no more than three seconds after entering the complaint.

The system should allow multiple users at once without any performance degradation.

Other routes except the public routes should authenticate using Clerk middleware.

File types like JPEG, PNG, and WebP, and file size not exceeding five megabytes should be validated upon uploading images.

The user interface must work well across all common desktop browsers.

Duplicate detection accuracy should have a false positive rate below five percent when employing complaints with urban density data.

MCIA should be recalculated within 200 milliseconds following the upvote event.











CHAPTER 2: PROFILE OF THE PROBLEM, RATIONALE AND SCOPE

2.1 INTRODUCTION TO THE PROBLEM

Complaints management for cities seems like an issue that’s already been cracked. All cities of decent size have some sort of portal/hotline system whereby citizens can register their grievances about infrastructure breakdowns. This is, of course, far from being a satisfactory process for those who have gone through it.

The fundamental problem here lies in scale and human intervention. A complaint lodged at 11 PM on a Friday is nothing more than an email sitting in a folder until Monday morning when the box is opened. Multiple citizens submitting the same complaint regarding the malfunctioning of a particular manhole will all be logged individually without mention of other cases of the same complaint. The operator responsible for processing hundreds of complaints during a single shift cannot make consistent judgments because it is impossible to make consistently categorical decisions in such large quantities.

This is not an anomaly but is commonplace within citizen complaint processes across the globe and has a very real outcome – namely, that citizens receiving no response stop submitting complaints, and thus the participation of citizens in the urban management process steadily disappears to the point where nobody benefits.

2.2 RATIONALE FOR THE PROPOSED SYSTEM

It should classify in three seconds maximum after the complaint entry.

It must run multiple users concurrently without affecting the application performance.

Except for the public routes, other authentication routes must use Clerk middleware.

Types like JPEG, PNG, WebP, and file size less than five megabytes should be checked while uploading pictures.

It must have good usability on most commonly used desktop web browsers.

Its accuracy in detecting duplicates must have an error rate of less than five percent while applying the complaints with urban density values.

Recalculate MCIA within 200 milliseconds after the upvote event.





2.3 PROBLEM STATEMENT

The present systems for handling citizens’ complaints are facing four interlinked drawbacks: manual and sluggish categorization leading to backlogged processing; unreliable estimation of threat level which leads to underestimating and misrouting serious threats; repetitive reports creating extra workloads without increasing capability for resolving issues; and inadequate visibility of the current situation making citizens lose their trust and interest in contributing. In this project, we develop and deploy an all-in-one solution that resolves all the above issues using artificial intelligence.

2.4 SCOPE OF THE STUDY

The scope of the project encompasses the entire complaint life cycle ranging from complaint submission by a citizen all the way to its completion by an administrator. The two-part reporting mechanism for a citizen that involves image uploading, geospatial duplicate detection using the Haversine formula for a 500-meter clustering radius, priority ranking using the MCIA model with real-time updating of the priority score upon upvotes, and administrative complaint management involving status updates, complaint departments assignment, and complaint note-taking forms part of this scope.

The current design accommodates web submission from the user’s desktop browser. Upcoming improvements involve development of a native mobile app for both iOS and Android, multi-language support in the complaint submission process, integration of IoT sensors for predictive maintenance, and API integration with municipal ERP systems.













CHAPTER 3: EXISTING SYSTEM

3.1 INTRODUCTION

Prior to the development of the system, a survey was carried out on the platforms that exist in order to gain insight on what has been created, what works, and where the critical gaps are. It is clear from the analysis that although there are several working platforms, no platform caters to the unique set of challenges faced by this initiative.

3.2 EXISTING SOFTWARE AND PLATFORMS

The most widespread system for civic complaints in North America and some parts of Europe is called SeeClickFix; it helps citizens to register their complaints via an app and channel them to the relevant city officials. It does include geolocation and basic categories of issues, while categorization itself takes place at the stage of user input, which results in frequent misrouting of the complaint to the wrong department.

The Fix My Street application is a popular application that follows this format: citizen report, citizen classify according to pre-defined geographic regions. The classification accuracy is determined by the citizen’s comprehension of the category definitions, which can vary significantly.

There are multiple state-level implementations of the Public Grievance Portal (PG Portal) in India that serve as the complaint handling portal for various government departments. They usually have users go through a complicated hierarchical categorization process, issue arbitrary reference numbers without any follow-up mechanism, and provide no real-time prioritization options to administrators other than FIFO queuing. Various academic implementations have focused on individual problem facets. Pothole images captured by cameras mounted on vehicles have been classified using CNNs  an entirely passive system that caters to only one type of infrastructure complaint and requires additional hardware.

3.3 DATA FLOW DIAGRAM FOR THE EXISTING SYSTEM

The traditional civic complaint handling system entails a simple yet inefficient process of transferring information. An ordinary citizen logs onto the website and fills in the form including both a description and a photograph of the complaint, if available. These details are recorded in the database for the complaints, with an assigned number. A municipal operator opens the inbox and reads through each complaint one-by-one and then categorizes them as per their pre-existing list and directs them to the concerned department. The department gets the case assigned to them, plans for its resolution, and at last solves the complaint – or leaves it unresolved, without any escalation system in place.

Weaknesses in the above flow:

The process of classification is subjective and unstandardized

There is nothing in the system to recognize that the two submissions have the same physical issue

Priority assignment is made based on arrival times

Citizen feedback is non-existent

3.4 WHAT IS NEW IN THE SYSTEM TO BE DEVELOPED

The AI-Empowered Urban Governance Platform stands out from other existing platforms in three important aspects.

Firstly, the process of categorization makes use of the generative multimodal AI model unlike in existing civic platforms where the process depends on citizen self-classification or classification with a specific domain classifier. Google Gemini 1.5 Flash has an ability to classify any kind of civic issue using natural language, incorporating both texts and images without need for any form of retraining. The current CNN classifiers require pre-trained data set models.

Secondly, the MCIA framework incorporates a composite priority score that has not been considered by any existing civic platform. Queues in existing platforms may be manually created or FIFO in nature. MCIA incorporates an AI-assessed severity of 40%, urgency of 30%, live community votes of 20% and a temporal recency weight of 10%.

Thirdly, automatic geospatial deduplication using the Haversine formula with a same category constraint is performed when the data is submitted. In current approaches, duplicate removal can be done manually by the operator or even not performed. Threshold for 500 meters radius was chosen according to block sizes found in most cities.

CHAPTER 4: PROBLEM ANALYSIS

4.1 PRODUCT DEFINITION

The product is a web application that allows citizens to file complaints against civic authorities; this product will be called the AI-Empowered Urban Governance System (AEUGS). It is developed using Next.js 15, and it uses a database of PostgreSQL running on NeonDB with an AI service based on Python Fast API microservices. There are two user types in AEUGS: citizen users and administrator users, who have different access levels.

The essential outputs include: Citizen-facing two-stage complaint submission system including picture attachment and GPS detection; AI categorization model based on Google’s Gemini 1.5 Flash; Geospatial duplicates detection algorithm employing Haversine formula; MCIA-based issue priority scoring algorithm with live recalculations; Citizen-oriented interface for issue tracking with six levels of status coloring; Administrative dashboard including issue priority queue, list of issues filtering, and analytical charts.

All of the required pieces are available as fully functional products or highly maintained open source software. The serverless PostgreSQL plan of NeonDB will easily handle data amounts anticipated in a demonstration. The authentication can be handled using a managed solution by Clerk without creating a new session solution from scratch.

The Gemini API provides data. However, scaling up this system for use on a city-wide level would require upgraded pricing from all services mentioned above, but that would only be proportional to the increased usage.

The citizen portal does not necessitate any technical skills. Only an image, a description, and an address are necessary to lodge a complaint. It works seamlessly using any standard internet browser.

5.3 SPECIFIC REQUIREMENTS

5.3.1 FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS

FR-01The system shall allow any visitor for register an account through email/password or social login via Clerk.

FR-02: Authenticated citizens should be able to register a complaint that has a title, description, one or more pictures (JPEG/PNG/WebP max 5MB each), and GPS location.

FR-03: GPS location is automatically obtained by the native Geolocation API on your browser, and there is reverse geocoding by OpenStreetMap Nominatim to display human-friendly address. There will be a fallback of manual latitude/longitude entry

FR-04: The AI classification call to Google Gemini 1.5 Flash shall be initiated for each complaint submission A valid JSON format is mandatory and the model will have to output category, severity, urgency, confidence score, description of analysis & tags.

FR-05: Before inserting a new complaint, system will search for all existing complaints within ±0.005° bounding box of GPS coordinates location and same AI-classified category of complaint Next, we will calculate the haversine distance for each candidate. If within 500m of the new submission, any candidate shall have the new submission considered a DUPLICATE and link this to the original via duplicateOfId.

FR-06: The system shall calculate an MCIA priority score for each complaint labeled as non-duplicate after AI classification is available.FR-07: There must be upvoting by citizens on every open complaint. The upvote operation is idempotent  a second upvote by the same user on the same complaint will take back the vote. The MCIA score of a complaint will be recalculated after every upvote or withdraw.

FR-08 : A citizen can view a list of all complaints made by him and their status, AI results and notes from the admin.

FR-09: As an administrator I want to see a priority queue for the top open complaints by MCIA score

FR-10 – The system will be able to display all complaints to the administrator, enabling him to filter (by status, category, severity) and sort (by priority, upvotes, date).

FR-11: The complaint detail page will allow the administrators to update complaint status, assign to a department, add notes and mark resolved.

FR-12: Summary statistics like Total Issues, Pending Number, In progress Number, Resolved number, Critical Nature Number and Total Registered Citizens must be displayed in administrative dashboard

FR-13: The system must allow for the display of analytics charts: monthly complaint trend (line), percentage by category (bar), status distribution chart (pie) & severity chart.

5.3.2 NON-FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS

NFR-01 Performance: End-to-end submission of a complaint (from filling one form to the AI analyzing it and writing the results to the database) should be performed within three seconds with varying network conditions.

NFR-02 Scalability: The serverless architecture with NeonDB and Vercel must be horizontally scalable without requiring code changes.

NFR-03 Security: Non-public routes must be secured with Clerk’s clerkMiddleware(). Citizens should not be able to reach any admin route implement role-based access control.

NFR-04 Reliability: All fields should have fallback defaults in Gemini API response validation so that the complaint pipeline does not fail due to a malformed AI response.

NFR-05 Usability A first time user must be able to complete the citizen reporting form in less than two minutes without instructions.

NFR-06 Accuracy: The accuracy of AI classification on the civic complaint categories should be greater than 85% as per empirical testing. The true positive rate of duplicate detection all should remain less than 5%.

NFR 07. Responsiveness: MCIA recalculation when upvoted, should finish in less than 200 milliseconds.

NFR-08 Data Integrity: The Upvote table should have a composite unique constraint on (issueId, userId) to ensure the same user cannot vote multiple times on the same complaint.

5.3.3 SYSTEM REQUIREMENTS

6.1 SYSTEM DESIGN

The system architecture is split into a collection of microservices across 3 layers: Next fullstack. Js web application that hosted a citizen-facing front end and back-end API routesJS; NeonDB PostgreSQL relational database accessed using Prisma ORM; a dedicated microservice (developed on Python FastAPI)One for processing AI requests, prioritising request scores, and detecting duplicate submissions. 6.2 DATABASE DESIGN

There are four models in the relational schema, namely; User, Issue, Upvote, and AiAnalysis. The User model maintains the Clerk-synchronized identity for every user and a role attribute that is either CITIZEN or ADMIN. The Issue model serves as the central model, which holds attributes such as the complaint’s title, description, picture URLs, GPS coordinates, status, number of upvotes, priority score, and two foreign keys for the user ID and parent issue (in case the issue exists multiple times). The Upvote model implements the uniqueness constraint, using a combination of issue ID and user ID, such that there can be only one vote by a user for a particular complaint.

6.4 DETAILED DESIGN

6.4.1 AI CLASSIFICATION PIPELINE

After getting the issue complaint, the /api/issues POST API causes the invocation of the function analyzeIssue() within src/lib/gemini.ts. As a consequence, the process leads to the creation of a well-formatted prompt consisting of the title, description, geolocation, and base64-encoded image (using inline data) of the complaint. The prompt requests the model to return a JSON object having six properties: category (civic categories), severity (LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH/CRITICAL), urgency (LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH/IMMEDIATE), confidence (0.0 - 1.0), analysis text (professional two-three sentences), and tags (two-five words).

6.4.2 MCIA PRIORITY SCORING

TPriority of a complaint is calculated by the Multimodal Civic Intelligence Algorithm using the following formula

P = (S × 0.40) + (U × 0.30) + (C × 0.20) + (R × 0.10)

Where:

S - severity

U - urgency

C - community engagement

R - recency

Severities are defined as follows:

LOW = 2.5

MEDIUM = 5.0

HIGH = 7.5

CRITICAL = 10.0

Similarly, urgencies are:

IMMEDIATE = 10.0

The community engagement component is computed as follows:

min( upvoteCount × 0.5, 10)

Community engagement is capped at a score of 10 to avoid any organized vote farming activity that can manipulate the queuing system. The recency component follows an exponential decay curve formula:

10 × e^(-age_in_days / 30)

This ensures that a report made recently will be granted around 10 points while a report filed 30 days ago gets roughly 3.7 points, ensuring that the older reports do not get buried for eternity without any advantage over the recent reports.

6.4.3 GEOSPATIAL DUPLICATE DETECTION

The query for searching is performed on all the records that have not yet been resolved and belong to the same type of AI, located at a distance of ±0.005° from the input GPS coordinates, prior to adding the new record. The formula used in the computation of the distance between two geographic locations using the Great Circle Distance is as follows:

a = sin²(Δφ/2) + cos(φ₁)·cos(φ₂)·sin²(Δλ/2); 

c = 2·arctan2(√a, √(1−a));

d = R·c where R = 6,371,000m.

When the value of d ≤ 500m, then the current record is added to the database with a DUPLICATE status and duplicateOfId refers to the existing record.

6.6.1 ANALYZE ISSUE (AI CLASSIFICATION)

FUNCTION analyzeIssue(title, description, lat, lng, imageBase64, existingIssues):

    prompt = BUILD_STRUCTURED_PROMPT(title, description, lat, lng)

    IF imageBase64 IS NOT NULL:

        content = [prompt, {inlineData: {data: imageBase64, mimeType: 'image/jpeg'}}]

    ELSE:

        content = [prompt]

    response = geminiModel.generateContent(content)

    result = PARSE_JSON(response.text)

    result = VALIDATE_AND_SANITIZE(result)  # fallback defaults

    FOR each issue IN existingIssues:

        IF issue.category == result.category:

            dist = haversine(lat, lng, issue.lat, issue.lng)

            IF dist <= 500:

                result.isDuplicate = TRUE

                result.duplicateOfId = issue.id

                BREAK

    RETURN result



6.6.2 CALCULATE MCIA SCORE

FUNCTION calculatePriorityScore(severity, urgency, upvoteCount, createdAt):

    S = SEVERITY_MAP[severity]   # LOW=2.5, MED=5, HIGH=7.5, CRIT=10

    U = URGENCY_MAP[urgency]     # LOW=2.5, MED=5, HIGH=7.5, IMM=10

    C = MIN(upvoteCount * 0.5, 10)

    ageDays = (NOW - createdAt).days

    R = 10 * EXP(-ageDays / 30)

    score = (S * 0.40) + (U * 0.30) + (C * 0.20) + (R * 0.10)

RETURN ROUND(score, 2)

In the second scenario, the second submission was made of the same category but with GPS coordinates that were 600 meters apart; hence a new complaint was created.

Idempotency of upvoting was tested through two successive votes made by one user account on the same issue. The upvoting process removed the previous vote, and the vote count decreased while the priority calculation made by MCIA recalculated.

Tests of the distance Haversine calculation included the use of coordinate pairs whose accuracy was checked externally. CHAPTER 8: IMPLEMENTATION

8.1 IMPLEMENTATION OF THE PROJECT

The project implementation was made in line with the phases described in Chapter 4. Each of those phases included adding some individual feature before moving onto another one. This allowed for the individual verification of all components and their integration as a whole into the project.

Regarding the Next.js 15 app, the first setup was performed using the create-next-app CLI tool together with the specified TypeScript App Router template. The data model schema was designed based on four defined entities (User, Issue, Upvote, AI Analysis) as well as relations between them before moving on to implement the other features.

Integrating AI technology was undoubtedly the most technically complicated part of the project. The function analyzeIssue() from src/lib/gemini.ts constructs a prompt that gets passed to the Gemini model using the @google/generative-ai library. Whenever there is an image next to a photo, the latter is appended as a second content item, passing its inline data along with the textual prompt, which means both the base64 encoding of the image and its MIME type. Multimodal aspect is thus defined by.

At the same time, another FastAPI Python microservice was developed. The service was planned to be run independently on another machine and would perform the same algorithmic operations as Next.js regarding MCIA and Haversine but be programmed in Python instead. This way, the purposeful AI layer was ensured. There were three endpoints specified: POST /analyze, for the full AI processing flow; POST /check-duplicate, for duplicate checking with regard to only geographical location; POST /priority, for the MCIA score calculation.

DashboardAdmin has been created by combining several backend components within the (admin) route category. The dashboard obtains the data that includes statistics, top 5 priorities queue and the latest issues in one HTTP request. Issue table displays all issues within the application but filters those with the help of AdminIssueFilters component. AnalyticsCharts uses four Recharts to show graphs for monthly number of complaints, number of complaints in each category, their status and severity level respectively.

All parts of the AI-Empowered Urban Governance System have been successfully deployed, tested, and released. 9.2 REMAINING AREAS OF CONCERN

Dependency on Google Gemini 1.5 Flash API: The dependency on Google Gemini 1.5 Flash API is there. Any changes in the cost structure or in the terms and conditions of the API would result in necessary changes in this pipeline. It would be best to have a more robust pipeline wherein the identities of the AI services provider remain hidden from its users.

Saving of Images: At present, the images are saved in the public/uploads/ folder of the filesystem. However, this needs to be changed for production and the use of object storage services such as Amazon S3 or Cloudflare R2 should be considered.

Mobility: The present design of the user interface is targeted at browsers running on the desktop operating system. Redesigning the user interface to make it accessible from a mobile device can allow the reporting process to be carried out through a mobile app.This will make it much easier for people who have problems accessing the web page from their mobile phones.

Language Options: All of the text on the prompt and the user interface are in English.

Asynchronous Processing: At present, the processing of API calls to Gemini for submitting complaints is synchronous. However, with high concurrent use of this process, there might be an increase in latency. Asynchronous processing would have to be implemented through placing the AI processing in the job queue.

Load Testing: At present, the system undergoes load testing using few users at one time. Testing of the system under heavy loads is yet to be conducted.

9.3 TECHNICAL AND MANAGERIAL LESSONS LEARNED 

TECHNICAL LESSONS:

Early schema-driven Prisma design turned out to be a great choice, as a clear data model defined in advance allowed us to avoid a wide variety of potential run-time errors and improved understanding of the whole system as a whole.

The process of structured prompt construction for Gemini proved to take longer than initially anticipated. Unstructured input generated output with variable formatting; the final version of prompt structure helped minimize the problem through clearly stated rules concerning JSON structure and enum options.

The Haversine algorithm was rather simple, but the use of the bounding box filtering technique helped considerably decrease the amount of costly distance computations needed to get results.

Serverless applications need extra attention paid to Prisma clients management. Utilizing singleton pattern for preventing connection pool exhaustion in Next.js serverless functions was a must in our case; otherwise, such an issue would arise in production.

MANAGERIAL LESSONS:

Partitioning the project into six distinct phases, each of which had its deliverables, was effective at organizing and tracking progress on a team of six people.

The decision to determine the database schema and TypeScript data types prior to forking into multiple streams helped to avoid integration issues due to the different pieces being independent.

Testing should always come first and not wait until the last phase. A few edge cases that came up while testing during Week 11 could have been resolved earlier, namely during Week 7.

Creating documentation while developing is better than doing it after the fact, both because it is more accurate and less time consuming.

FIGURE 7: ADMIN ISSUE MANAGEMENT FLOWCHART

The work flow of the administrator managing complaints is shown in Figure7. This is followed by admin capable of role based authentication, viewing MCIA sorted priority queue and filtering issues/ selecting issues to act on through assigning departments, updating status, also with the ability to add notes or mark resolved. All actions will continuously update the database and the citizens tracking page in real time.



Figure 7: Admin Issue Management Flowchart — From login to issue resolution



FIGURE 8: MCIA PRIORITY SCORING ALGORITHM FLOWCHART

The MCIA algorithm is shown in Figure 8. All four components Severity (S), Urgency (U), Community (C), and Recency (R) are quantified separately, then combined through a weighted calculation: P = (S×0.40)+(U×0.30)+(C×0.20)+(R×0.10). The outcome then gets mapped to one of four different priority labels. A citizen upvote re-calculates the community component and re-computes the full score to keep the admin queue fresh.



Figure 8: MCIA Algorithm Flowchart — Weighted priority scoring with real-time upvote recalculation
